# Scan Results as Provenance: How Xray and AppTrust Meet

This document is a mental model, not a procedure. It explains the seam between two JFrog subsystems
that are frequently described as if they were one: **Xray**, which analyses binaries and produces
findings, and **AppTrust**, which assembles application versions and moves them through a
lifecycle. The thing that spans the seam is **Evidence** — signed, subject-bound statements that
something was observed or asserted about a specific artifact.

The organising spine is one question followed end to end: what happens to a CycloneDX software bill
of materials when it stops being a scan report and becomes an attestation attached to an
application version, and what that change makes possible during promotion from one lifecycle stage
to the next. If you need to reason about where a gate should live, which subject a claim should
attach to, or why a scan that definitely ran does not satisfy a rule that definitely exists, this
is the model to reason with.

## 1. Two planes: analysis and attestation

The central question is deceptively simple: **does Xray scan data become Evidence automatically
once a package is scanned, or must it be deliberately attached?** It is worth asking not because
the answer is hard to look up, but because asking it forces the two planes apart.

The **analysis plane** is Xray. It produces findings: vulnerabilities, license issues, contextual
analysis, exposures. A finding is an *observation about an artifact at a point in time*, and it is
inherently mutable — the same binary rescanned next week against a refreshed vulnerability database
yields a different answer, and that is the correct behaviour. Analysis is meant to track the world
as the world changes.

The **attestation plane** is Evidence. It produces signed statements: this predicate, about this
subject, from this provider, at this moment, cryptographically bound. An attestation is *immutable
by design*. It does not claim that the artifact is safe; it claims that a particular observation
was made, by a particular party, about a particular artifact. Its value comes precisely from the
fact that it does not change when the world does.

Conflating the two is the root of most confusion here. "The image was scanned and came back clean"
is an analysis-plane statement with no shelf life. "On this date, this provider asserted this
inventory for this digest" is an attestation-plane statement that remains true forever, and is
therefore the only kind that can meaningfully travel with a release.

### What crosses the seam automatically

Xray does generate evidence internally, but only at specific composition or promotion triggers —
when a release-level object is created and scanned, or when an application version is promoted. In
the AppTrust case, promotion of an application version results in the creation of additional
evidence such as scan results and a bill of materials. Promotion also records a signed promotion
attestation, though that one originates in Artifactory rather than in Xray.

No documented trigger describes a standalone package scan producing evidence on its own. The
reasonable inference is that scanning a package in isolation yields findings but no attestation —
but that is an inference drawn from the absence of a documented trigger, not a documented
statement, and it is worth treating as unresolved rather than settled.

The platform's own vocabulary reinforces the split. It does not speak of "system" or "built-in"
evidence; it distinguishes **internal evidence**, generated by the platform's own services, from
**external evidence**, uploaded by anyone else. That is a provenance distinction, not a quality
one: it records who made the claim, which is what an attestation is for.

Producing an attestation from analysis output is therefore a deliberate act. There is no generic
conversion path turning arbitrary scanner output into a predicate, which is coherent rather than an
omission: internal analysis writes its own evidence directly, so a conversion path is only needed
for external producers, and exists only for the specific external tools the platform has
integrated. Everything else — including binding a bill of materials that Xray itself produced to a
subject as a signed claim — is an explicit act of the pipeline. That is a design decision, not a
chore: you choose which analysis output is important enough to become permanent record, and what
subject it describes.

## 2. Analysis has its own scope model, and it is opt-in

Xray does not observe everything. A resource is analysed only once it has been explicitly declared
in scope — indexing is a prerequisite for scanning, and the platform does not automatically scan
all repositories, builds, or artifacts unless they are explicitly indexed. Scope is declared per
resource type; repositories, builds, and release-level objects are distinct kinds of thing, and
putting one in scope does not put the others in scope. Separately, findings only become
**violations** when a watch binds an analysed resource to a policy. Indexing decides *what gets
looked at*; watches decide *what the findings mean*.

This matters because the analysis plane's scope model is entirely independent of the attestation
plane's. Evidence attaches to a subject regardless of whether that subject was ever indexed;
conversely, a resource can be thoroughly analysed and produce no attestation at all. The two planes
share no notion of coverage, so an architect reasoning about a "scanned and governed" pipeline is
really reasoning about two overlapping scopes that must be arranged to coincide.

It also explains a structural difference in how the two kinds of enforcement degrade. A gate
depending on live analysis is only as reliable as the analysis service is reachable at the moment
of promotion; the platform even offers a configuration in which promotion proceeds when Xray is
enabled but unreachable. A gate evaluating recorded evidence asks about the past, and the past is
still there when a service is not.

## 3. Predicate types are the contract between producer and consumer

An evidence record carries a **predicate** — the payload — and a **predicate type**, a URI
identifying what kind of claim the payload is. The predicate type is not a label; it is the
interface. The producer writes it to declare what is being asserted, and the consumer — a gate
rule, a graph view, a downstream verifier — matches on that exact string to decide whether this
record is the kind of claim it cares about. Nothing else in the record performs that function. A
perfectly valid bill of materials attached under a type nobody matches on is a claim that exists,
is signed, is verifiable, and is nevertheless *unconsumable*. It does not fail loudly; it simply
never satisfies anything.

Because the match is on an exact string, version suffixes in the URI are part of the contract too.
A record published as `.../cyclonedx/sbom/v1.4` and a rule keyed on `.../cyclonedx/sbom/v1.6`
describe the same concept and do not interoperate. Alignment between what producers emit and what
rules expect is the most consequential configuration in the whole model, and the piece with the
least feedback when it is wrong.

### Categories make the graph navigable

Predicate types are grouped into five categories:

| Category | What it groups |
|---|---|
| **Security** | Vulnerability findings, bills of materials, and vulnerability-exploitability statements |
| **Quality** | Test results, static-analysis and code-quality results, integration test outcomes |
| **Workflow** | Build information, promotion and distribution records, signatures, provenance attestations |
| **Audit** | Approvals, both human and automated, and review or committer attestations |
| **Custom** | The fallback bucket for any predicate type the platform does not recognise |

Categories are a queryable taxonomy layered over the type contract, and their purpose is
navigability: with many records hanging off the objects in a release, "what security claims exist
about this version?" needs to be answerable without knowing every type URI in advance.

The Custom bucket is the tell. An unregistered predicate type still works as a contract — a rule
keyed on that exact string will match it — but it is invisible to any category-level view.
Registering your own types under the appropriate category is what makes a hand-attached claim
legible to someone *reading* the graph rather than querying it. Registration is an assignment
layered on top of the built-in mapping, so the built-in types remain in place whether or not you
add your own.

## 4. The evidence graph links; it does not copy

This is the architectural crux, and most practical decisions descend from it. An AppTrust
application version is a **composite**: it groups artifacts, packages, builds, release-level
objects, and even other application versions as its sources. Creating that version does **not**
copy the evidence attached to its constituents. The relationship is graph linkage. The version sits
at the top with its own evidence beneath it; the constituent builds and packages sit alongside,
each retaining its own evidence; and the whole thing is reachable as one structure from the
version.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions. **Evidence attached to a constituent
remains reachable from every version that includes it** — a bill of materials attached to a package
is a fact about that package, attached once when the package is produced and amortised across every
application version that ever incorporates it. **Evidence attached to a version describes that
specific assembly** — an approval or a composed inventory is a claim about *this arrangement of
these constituents*, and has no meaning for any other version.

So the subject you choose determines both the **scope** of the claim and its **lifetime**:

| Attachment subject | Scope of the claim | When it is the right choice |
|---|---|---|
| Package or artifact | The binary itself, wherever it appears | Facts intrinsic to the component — its composition, its findings at build time |
| Build | One execution that produced outputs | Facts about how something was produced — inputs, environment, provenance of the build |
| Application version | One assembled release | Facts about the release as a whole — approvals, composed inventory, stage transitions |

The practical shape this implies is sequential rather than either/or: attach to packages and builds
**as they are produced**, because that is when the facts are known and the application version does
not exist yet; then attach to the application version **once it exists**, for anything that is a
fact about the assembly. The graph composes the two.

One wrinkle follows directly from linkage, and it is the most common source of a rule that never
fires. Rule evaluation is scoped: a rule asks whether a claim exists on the *evaluated resource*,
and depending on the rule, possibly on its parent application. Where a claim sits therefore
determines whether a given rule can see it. Evidence created before an application version exists
is initially unassigned to any version and joins the version's graph through composition rather
than by being moved. Linkage is what makes that work — and also what makes "it is in the graph
somewhere" an insufficient answer to "does the gate see it?"

## 5. A bill of materials as provenance, not as a report

Everything above converges here.

A bill of materials produced as scan output is a **report**: generated on demand, regenerable at
any time, carrying no inherent claim about who produced it or when. Regenerate it in six months and
it may differ, and nothing in the document tells you whether it corresponds to the binary you
shipped. The same document attached as signed evidence to a subject is an **attestation**: a
statement that this inventory was asserted, by this party, about this exact digest, at this moment.
The content is identical; what changed is that the document is now *bound* — to a subject, a
signer, and a point in time. Verifying an unbound bill of materials tells you only that a file
parses. Verifying a bound one tells you that a specific party stood behind a specific inventory for
a specific binary. Binding is what makes later verification mean anything.

Binding at a point in time also makes the document honest about its own limits. An inventory is a
*composition* claim, and composition does not change after the artifact is built. Vulnerability
knowledge does change, which is why exploitability statements are carried as their own kind of
claim alongside the inventory rather than baked into it. That is the section 1 distinction
expressed in the data model: the inventory is a durable fact, the vulnerability assessment is a
dated observation, and keeping them separate lets each be re-evaluated on its own schedule.

Relatedly, a bill of materials in this format carries vulnerabilities but not *violations* — a
correct boundary rather than a limitation. A vulnerability is a fact about the world; a violation
is a verdict relative to a policy, and policies differ across organisations and change over time.
Verdicts do not travel well as provenance; facts do.

### Travelling through promotion

An application version moves through the lifecycle — for example `DEV`, then `QA`, then `STAGING`,
then `PROD`. Because evidence is bound to subjects and the graph links rather than copies, the
accumulated attestations travel with the version by construction. Nothing is re-attached at each
stage; the graph is simply reachable from the version wherever it goes.

This is what turns each promotion into an **evaluation point rather than a re-derivation point**.
The question at a stage boundary is not "scan this again and tell me what you find" but "what has
already been asserted about this version and its constituents, and is it sufficient to enter this
stage?" Analysis happened once, when it was cheapest and most accurate — at build time, with full
context. Enforcement happens many times, at every boundary, using the record of that analysis.

Promotion also *adds* to the graph: the platform records the transition itself as signed evidence,
and internal scan and inventory evidence is generated at promotion. The version's graph therefore
grows monotonically as it advances, and by the final stage it carries a signed, ordered account of
everything asserted about it along the way. That account, not any individual scan, is the release's
provenance.

## 6. Gating is policy over the evidence graph

Enforcement composes from three objects: a **template** expressing reusable evaluation logic with
declared parameters, a **rule** fixing concrete values for those parameters, and a **policy**
binding the rule to a scope, a lifecycle stage boundary, and an action. The scope may be global, an
entire project, or a single application; the boundary is the entry to or exit from a stage.

The conceptual content of a typical gate is narrow, deliberately so. It asks: *has this claim been
made — of this predicate type, from this provider, about this resource or its parent application?*
It does not re-run analysis and does not inspect the payload for a verdict. It asks about the
existence and the provenance of an attestation.

That narrowness is the whole point, and it is the actual integration point between Xray and
AppTrust. **Gating on evidence decouples when analysis happens from when it is enforced.** The scan
runs once, in the pipeline, where the source, the build context, and the toolchain are all
available. Enforcement runs at every stage boundary thereafter, in a system that never needs to
know how the scan was performed, and neither side needs to be available when the other runs. That
decoupling is what lets a governance model survive tool changes, scanner upgrades, and pipelines it
was not designed around.

It also explains why section 3's exact type match matters so much. The gate's entire view of a
claim is its type, its provider, and its subject; everything else is payload the gate does not
read. And because one rule expresses one condition, requiring several independent claims at a
boundary means several policies rather than one compound rule — gates decompose into atomic,
individually reportable conditions rather than a single pass/fail expression, which is better for
diagnosis and worse for brevity.

### Warning and blocking as a rollout strategy

A policy runs in either **warning** or **blocking** mode, best understood as a sequencing tool
rather than a severity setting. In warning mode the gate evaluates fully and reports its verdict,
but promotion proceeds — which is how you learn whether the contract in section 3 is actually
aligned, whether the type your producers emit is the type your rules match, without a release being
blocked by a misconfiguration. Blocking mode turns the same evaluation into an enforcement
boundary. The transition between the two deserves to be a deliberate decision, separate from the
decision to write the rule, because the failure modes differ entirely: a wrong rule in warning mode
produces a misleading report, and the same rule in blocking mode stops a release.

One corollary is easy to miss. A pipeline that passes proves nothing about a gate: it is equally
consistent with the gate working, with the gate matching nothing, and with the gate never being
evaluated. Only a promotion that is correctly refused demonstrates that a gate is real, so the
refusal is the thing worth testing.

## 7. Why application versions rather than release-level bundles

Both the older release-bundle model and the newer application-version model can act as the subject
of release-level evidence, and both integrate with analysis. They are not equivalent in direction
of travel: application versions are where the platform's governance model is being developed, and
release bundles are on a published path out.

This creates a genuine pull in the wrong direction when learning the model, because the clearest
documentation of internally generated scan evidence lives on the release-bundle pages: the
mechanism is described best in the place you should not build on. The conceptual content transfers
— composition, linkage, internally generated inventory and scan evidence at a release-level trigger
— but the object to build on is the application version.

The deeper reason is that the application version is the richer abstraction. It can compose other
application versions, carries an application-level identity that gate scopes can target, and moves
through a lifecycle as a first-class concept rather than being distributed as an artifact set.
Provenance meant to accumulate across an application's history needs an object with a history, and
that is what the application version is.

## 8. Open questions

These remain genuinely open in this model. They are not lookups waiting to be done; each one would
change how a pipeline should be designed, and each has a defensible answer either way.

**Does analysis alone ever produce an attestation?** Every documented trigger for internally
generated evidence is a release-level composition or a promotion. Whether a package scan in
isolation produces a record is unstated. If it does, package-level evidence accumulates passively
and pipelines need attach less; if it does not, every durable claim about a package is something a
pipeline must deliberately make.

**Which specification version does internally generated inventory evidence actually carry?**
Category registrations and out-of-the-box rules do not obviously agree on a single version of the
bill-of-materials predicate type, and per section 3, an exact-string mismatch is a rule that
silently never fires. This is the question with the largest gap between how trivial it looks and
how much depends on it.

**Where does promotion-triggered evidence attach — to the application version, or to its
constituents?** Section 4's reasoning about subject, scope, and lifetime applies to the platform's
own evidence exactly as it applies to yours. The answer determines which rules can see it, and
whether observing the constituents is enough to observe everything that happened.

**Does registering a custom predicate type under a category reclassify records already attached
under it, or only those attached afterwards?** This is the difference between category assignment
being a property of the record at write time and a property of the type at read time, and it
determines whether taxonomy can be corrected after the fact.
